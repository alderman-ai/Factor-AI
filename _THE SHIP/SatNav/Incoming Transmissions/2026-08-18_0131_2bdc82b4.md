Read it. There's a real economy in here — the resource→skill mapping is genuinely good, and a few of these mechanics are better than you probably realize. But there are three load-bearing failures, and one of them is the kind this project's own rules tell me to stop you on.

## The three that break it

**1. You made the fence purchasable.**

Iron plate buys "body armor access/hacking toolhooks." Stone brick buys "defensive tool hooks." So spending resources lets you edit PreToolUse hooks — and PreToolUse hooks are the thing enforcing the prose lockdown and the resource ledger itself. Any player who can afford armor can afford to legislate their own constraints away. The fence is inside the game.

The enforcement layer has to sit *outside* the economy. You can buy the right to *add* hooks; you can never buy write access to the ones that meter you. That's a hard partition you need to draw explicitly, because otherwise the first time the economy bites, the cheapest move is to buy your way out — and it'll look like legitimate play.

**2. Power is self-reported, so it isn't a constraint.**

"Cap estimated token spend per minute" — estimated by whom? If I'm the one reporting my own token spend, power is a vibe, not a meter. A resource nobody can measure is a resource nobody can run out of. Power has to come from a deterministic read (a hook summing actual usage out of the session transcript) or the entire power economy — which is load-bearing for coal, wood, copper, and condensed fuel — is decorative.

**3. The economy dies after the build phase.**

You've collapsed two different currencies into one. Some resources buy *permanent artifacts* (variables, skills, templates, hooks) — that's capex, spent once, kept forever. Others buy *consumption* (power/tokens) — opex, spent continuously. Both go through the same 5:1 smelter.

Consequence: variables and skills accumulate monotonically and never cost anything to hold or run. Fifteen sessions in, you have an enormous unlocked pool, prose is no longer scarce because your templates cover everything, and the constraint has quietly stopped biting. Nothing in the doc makes owning things expensive. Factorio's answer is that every built thing draws power forever — you may want that: unlocked variables and skills have an upkeep, so breadth costs you throughput.

## Two exploits waiting to happen

**Power has four uncosted inlets.** Coal → power. Wood → power transport. Copper → "increases power available." Condensed fuel → 10x power. No exchange rates stated. Whichever path is cheapest becomes the only path anyone ever uses, and the other three become dead content. Either publish the rates or make power a single hard-capped ledger that no combination of inputs can exceed.

**Water = CLAUDE.md prose.** In Factorio water is functionally infinite — offshore pump, no depletion. If you keep canon, CLAUDE.md prose is unlimited and line 1 of your own doc is void. Either water gets metered against canon (finite tank, refilled only by lab research) or prose isn't water.

## Undefined — your calls, not mine

- **Biters have no spawn source.** You've built demand for defense with no supply of threat. Factorio's loop is *consumption → pollution → attacks → defense spend*. You need the pollution analog. Uncommitted files, failed validations, token overspend, dead artifacts — something your own activity generates. Without it, all defensive spend is irrational.
- **No clock, no depletion, no starting inventory.** "Limited, but not indefinitely" needs a tick. Session? Commit? Day? And does anything deplete, or is it pure accumulation?
- **Uniform 5:1 smelting removes the puzzle.** In Factorio the *differing* ratios are where the thinking is. One flat rate means every secondary costs the same, so there's no tradeoff to reason about.
- **Assembler 2 and 3 have no build path**, and "task / build / project" aren't defined against each other. Also: hand-build costs coal, but coal is "tier 1 human skill" — so is coal earned by demonstrating skill and spent by acting? That's coherent if stated, ambiguous as written.
- **Steam → nuclear is a progression, not a resource.** It's sitting in the primaries list.

## The buried lede

Your transport tier is the most interesting thing in the document and the least developed. Inserter = local, belt = one-shot anywhere, roboport = unlimited on cooldown — transport *of what?* Files in a repo are already globally readable. The only thing that actually needs transporting is **context**: which artifact's contents may be loaded into which session. That maps directly onto your stated improvement goals — context management, and clobber prevention across parallel sessions on one repo without worktrees.

Which raises the question the doc never asks: **where does the ledger live, and what serializes writes to it?** You've committed to multiple parallel sessions seeing the same state, no worktrees. A shared mutable resource ledger under those conditions is the single hardest engineering problem in this design, and it's the one you'll learn the most from.

Two things I need from you before I'd build any of this: where the ledger file lives and what its format is, and whether you want the capex/opex split (upkeep on owned artifacts) or something else to keep the economy alive past the build phase.

## The gap that decides whether belts matter at all

**You've defined cross-fence *write*. You haven't defined cross-fence *read*.**

If a session in Assembler can `Read` the Lab's ledger directly, the belt economy is cosmetic — information is the actual payload here, and you'd only ever need a belt to *deposit*, never to *learn*. Everything interesting gets copied by eye and the belts move nothing that matters.

Factorio already answered this: you can't see a distant chest either. You need **radar, and radar costs power**. That gives you a clean two-axis system you're most of the way to already:

- **Belt** — cross-fence *write*, consumed, one artifact, frontmatter-gated
- **Radar** — cross-fence *read*, costs power per look, no artifact moves

That also finally gives power a mandatory sink that isn't self-reported, and it makes "can I afford a mining station and a radar open at the same time" — your own line 11 — mechanically true instead of flavor.

## The fence is thinner than it looks

A path-prefix check will not hold, especially on Windows:

- `..\Lab\ledger.md` resolves outside the fence but string-matches inside it
- NTFS junctions and symlinks, UNC paths, 8.3 short names, backslash vs forward slash, case-insensitivity
- **Read/Edit/Write are not the only doors.** Bash and PowerShell (`cat`, `Get-Content`, `robocopy`), and `Grep`/`Glob` with a `path` argument, all reach the filesystem. A hook that only guards the file tools leaves the shell wide open.

The fence has to enforce on the **resolved absolute path** for **every** tool that can touch disk, shell included. That's one hook with a real path-canonicalization step, not a matcher per tool.

## Who is allowed to write the fences?

If each component's fence lives in that component's own `.claude/settings.json`, a session inside it can amend its own fence — the armor problem again, one floor down.

So the fences live in the parent. But the parent `Factor-AI` is also a Claude working directory, which makes any parent session god-mode with the power to quietly undo the whole economy. You need one of: a parent whose own hook forbids editing hook config (a fence guarding itself), or an explicit rule that the parent is never a play surface — only a referee. **This is a call you have to make, and it's the single most load-bearing one in the design.**

## The global belt ledger is your clobber problem, sharpened

A belt is consumed, so consumption must be recorded somewhere both parties trust. But:

- In the **sender's local ledger** → the sender can lie, or crash mid-transfer
- In a **global ledger** → that file is outside every fence, so no component can legally decrement it

The way out is that the **transfer skill is a privileged broker**: it runs outside the session's permission surface and is the *only* writer to the global ledger. Worth naming that explicitly in the design, because everything else rests on it.

And make the artifact its own receipt — the delivered file's frontmatter records the belt spend. Then a half-completed transfer is *detectable by scanning*, not something you have to trust a session's word about. Order the steps so the ledger decrements last, and reconciliation becomes arithmetic.

## Three unresolved rules, in descending sharpness

**Does a failed frontmatter validation burn the belt?** My recommendation: yes, burn it. Free retries train nothing and turn the validator into a guess-until-it-passes loop — exactly the "write something designed to pass" failure your CLAUDE.md prohibits. Burning it makes malformed headers cost a real resource, which is the whole point of the constraint.

**Belt supply and cooldown are unstated.** "One at a time" is currently a syntax restriction, not a throughput limit — if you can lay the next belt instantly, there's no scarcity. Your doc puts wood at "power transport," which reads like wood is the belt material. If so, say it, and say the rate.

Then it's a bootstrap phase, not an economy — scarcity as a discovery instrument, retired once it's done its job. That's a legitimate strategy and it reframes what "dies out" needs to mean. Which surfaces a different set of problems.

## The scaffolding's product is a trap registry — and nothing currently writes it

If the point is to surface friction so later execution avoids it, then **every block has to become a durable record**, not just an experience. Right now nothing in the design captures a failure as data. A hook denial happens, you see it in a terminal, the session ends, and the lesson lives in a transcript nobody re-reads. When the scaffolding comes down, its entire output is gone.

Every fence denial and failed validation should append to a log: what was attempted, which fence, the resolved path, what the legal move would have been. **That log is the deliverable.** The belts and the power ledger are disposable — the registry is the thing you carry into real execution.

And it closes the loop your own doc opened: research questions feed raw docs to ShipNav, then the Lab compiles them into KB entries. The raw docs are your own failures. You already built the pipeline for this; you just haven't pointed it at the friction.

## Fences and resources must have different lifespans

You're treating these as one thing, and they shouldn't be:

- **Resource scarcity** — belts, power, coal. Scaffolding. Dies at graduation. Correct.
- **Fences** — directory isolation, frontmatter validation, one-artifact-per-move. **Not scaffolding. That's the architecture.**

If the fences retire alongside the belts, every route you validated stops being enforced and you're back to a monolith with good intentions and a nice log file. The correct demolition is: transfers become **free, but still legal-only**. Unlimited belts, identical contract. Remove the *cost*, keep the *shape*.

Worth stating explicitly, because "the early economy dies out" reads naturally as "the constraints come off," and that reading would throw away the build.

## Scarcity will teach you distorted paths if you let it bite on shape

This is the trap I'd worry about most, and it's one every Factorio player has lived. When belts are scarce you optimize for belt count — batch three artifacts into one file, flatten your frontmatter, denormalize to save a trip. Those workarounds then get tested, cached, and inherited as "the common path." Late game runs on a schema shaped by a constraint that no longer exists. It's the spaghetti you laid down when you had twelve iron plates and are still untangling at 10k SPM.

So the one-artifact-one-move contract has to be **non-negotiable and never relaxable by scarcity**. Scarcity bites on *rate* — how often you can ship — never on *shape*. Otherwise the bootstrap phase's main output is bad habits with test coverage.

## Graduation needs a coverage criterion, not a vibe

Two ways this ends badly: it never formally ends and you're paying belt tax at hour 200 out of habit, or it ends by attrition — you get tired of it, quietly stop enforcing, and never harvest the lesson because there was no moment of harvest.

Give it a **legible exit**: every component-to-component edge you'll actually use has shipped at least one artifact successfully, with a validated frontmatter contract recorded per edge. That's a matrix, component × component, generated *from the denial/transfer log* rather than maintained by hand. It tells you which edges are still untested — which is the thing you will otherwise never notice until real execution hits a cold path at the worst moment.

Note the dependency: no log, no matrix, no graduation condition. It all hangs off the first section.

## One practical hazard in the bootstrap phase itself

Early on, a denial is ambiguous: either you did an illegal thing, or the hook has a path-normalization bug. Opposite correct responses — learn from one, fix the other. Without a way to tell them apart you'll either patch real fences (defeating the purpose) or work around real bugs (poisoning the registry with fake traps).

You need a **contested-denial state**: a way to flag "I believe this block was incorrect" that queues it for review without routing around it. That keeps your CLAUDE.md rule intact — the fence still holds, the failure is still shown — while giving genuine bugs a path that isn't "quietly disable the hook."
